An Invitation to the First Students

Philosophy as a way of life. An invitation to learn and work together.

Ramon Eyquem

Philosophy Is Not Only Knowledge, but Life

I am addressing people who want not merely to read about philosophy, but to practice it and live according to their convictions. I am establishing my own philosophical school and looking for its first students—people willing to learn, think independently, and help me develop the international philosophical community Homo Provincialis.

To me, philosophy is not a list of names and doctrines to memorize. Its task is to investigate reality, understand human beings, seek truth, create ideas, and put them into practice. A teacher of philosophy should not only speak about principles, but strive to demonstrate them through personal conduct.

I especially admire Epicurus and Diogenes of Sinope because they showed how philosophy can shape a way of life. Epicurus's Garden reminds us of philosophy as shared inquiry, friendship, and everyday practice. Diogenes exemplifies independence from convention and consistency in life. I do not propose copying their doctrines: I am developing my own views, and I want students to learn to think for themselves.

The Foundations of My Philosophy

In metaphysics, I hold to materialism and hard determinism. I believe we should rely on science, rational investigation, and the testing of claims rather than authority or unsupported belief. I support scientific and social progress.

In my understanding of human beings and society, humanism and altruism are important. In political philosophy, I defend voluntarism: human relations and cooperation should be based on free choice rather than imposed submission. I value the idea of a community of free people united not by a state or coercion, but by shared interests, mutual assistance, and voluntary choice.

In moral philosophy, I am critical of traditional ethics when it becomes a ready-made set of doctrines and authoritative formulas. This does not mean rejecting morality—quite the opposite. I regard the investigation of human morality as one of philosophy's most important tasks. My project Human Morality examines moral principles, justice, and human actions. Its propositions should be subjected to rigorous inquiry, not accepted on faith.

Virtue Comes First

For me, one of the most important questions is virtue. A person may be educated and possess knowledge and abilities, but none of this replaces moral character. Without virtue, knowledge alone does not make someone a good person.

I therefore want our philosophical school to unite inquiry with life. We should not only discuss goodness, justice, compassion, solidarity, and love, but investigate how these qualities appear in real relationships and actions. Philosophy cannot be considered complete where words and conduct contradict each other.

This standard applies first of all to me as a teacher. I do not claim to be incapable of error. If a student convincingly shows that I am wrong, I will acknowledge it. Truth matters more than anyone's name, position, or authority.

What You Can Learn Here

Universities and books can provide extensive knowledge of the history of philosophy. That knowledge is valuable, but familiarity with other people's views does not itself amount to creating a philosophy of one's own. I offer a different emphasis: studying philosophical questions with someone developing an original teaching, and taking part in its critical examination and development.

We will learn to define concepts clearly, distinguish arguments from assertions, examine the grounds of our beliefs, and build coherent reasoning. Equally important, we will test philosophy in life: how we treat other people, how we cooperate, how we resolve disagreements, and whether our actions reflect our principles.

I am not looking for people who merely repeat my words. I want to share methods of philosophical investigation and search for truth together with students. In time, a student may develop original ideas and become a teacher as well.

What We Will Build

Homo Provincialis is envisioned as an international, voluntary philosophical community. Its participants may live in different countries, communicate, learn, investigate, create, and help one another. It does not require a state of its own or a single place of residence.

There are already areas for collaboration: philosophical essays and research, the Human Morality project, an open online library, translations of philosophical books, the development of a universal philosophical language, and other initiatives. You can choose work suited to your interests and abilities. We will develop these projects gradually, as people and opportunities emerge.

I envision a philosophical community in which study, scientific work, art, friendship, and daily life are connected. But I do not expect a future student to immediately relocate or change their entire way of life. We can begin with a conversation and a shared inquiry.

Whom I Invite

I am speaking to someone interested in seeking truth and developing intellectual and moral qualities. You do not need a philosophy degree, knowledge of ancient languages, or agreement with every one of my views. What matters more is the ability to listen, understand, ask questions, justify conclusions, and honestly acknowledge mistakes.

Being a student does not mean worshipping a teacher. It means engaging seriously with my ideas, working together, and being willing to test them through reason and experience. Mutual respect and voluntary participation are essential. We can begin in person during my travels or online; English is intended as the common language of the international school.

I do not offer a diploma, paid employment, or guaranteed income. I offer the opportunity to learn philosophy as a way of life and to take part in building a new philosophical community from its beginnings.

How to Begin

If these aims appeal to you, explore my philosophical writings and the Homo Provincialis projects. Consider which ideas interest you, which questions you want to investigate, and what you might contribute to our work.

Write to me about yourself and why you want to pursue philosophy. You need not make a grand declaration or immediately decide to become a student. We can begin with a conversation, try to understand one another, and see whether we can learn and create together.

I invite the first students not to a finished institution, but to the beginnings of a philosophical school in which the search for truth, virtue, and philosophy as a way of life are meant to be practices, not slogans.
