An Invitation to My First Student

An invitation to those who wish to study philosophy and build a philosophical community

Ramon Eyquem

I want to establish my own philosophical school, and I am looking for someone with whom I can begin this work. Perhaps that person is you.

I do not yet have a school building, a timetable, or an established community of students. I have my philosophical views, ideas, and the Homo Provincialis projects on which I am working. As I travel, I hope to meet people for whom philosophy is not merely a subject to study but an important part of life.

What I Mean by Philosophy

Knowing the history of philosophy is useful, but knowing other people's doctrines is not enough. To me, a philosopher is someone who independently investigates the world, develops a worldview, and proposes and defends ideas. Philosophy is a creative pursuit and a search for truth, not simply a repetition of what others have said.

I am developing my own philosophical teaching. My school will therefore not be a discussion club in which every incompatible claim is treated as equally correct. We must distinguish knowledge from conjecture and unsupported opinion, examine arguments, and refuse to accept claims merely because someone wishes to believe them.

This standard applies to me as well. If a student demonstrates that I am wrong and supports the conclusion with sound reasoning, I will acknowledge it. Truth matters more to me than the authority of a teacher. But disagreement alone is not an argument: one must explain why a claim is false.

Whom I Am Looking For

Above all, I am looking for one person who genuinely wants to engage seriously with philosophy. You do not need a degree, extensive knowledge of philosophical names, or prior agreement with all my views. Curiosity, the ability to reason, and a willingness to read, question, examine arguments, and revise beliefs when warranted matter more.

I am not looking for an admirer or someone who merely repeats my words. Nor am I looking for someone who argues for the sake of arguing, refuses to consider reasons, or treats every personal belief as beyond examination. I hope to meet someone interested in understanding my philosophical teaching and contributing to its development.

We may disagree on particular questions. What matters is a shared commitment to the search for truth, intellectual integrity, and voluntary cooperation.

What We Could Do Together

We can begin with ordinary conversations, either in person during my travels or online. We can discuss philosophical problems, read and investigate texts, examine my ideas, test arguments, and gradually discover whether we wish to continue working together.

If there is mutual interest, you could participate in the development of Homo Provincialis, an international philosophical community. Its projects include philosophical publications, an open online library, book translation, education, and other forms of research. No one needs to work on everything at once: participation depends on each person's abilities, interests, and free choice.

In the longer term, I envision a community whose members not only study philosophy but help one another learn, investigate, create ideas, and pass knowledge to future students. The Garden of Epicurus is an important historical example for me: philosophy as a shared activity and way of life. The precise form of our school is not yet fixed; it can emerge through our work.

What I Am Not Offering

I do not promise a diploma, income, accommodation, employment, or a ready-made career. I am not asking you to join a closed organization, submit to my person, or abandon independent thought. At first, I am proposing an introduction, philosophical conversations, and the possibility of voluntary cooperation.

Nor do I expect a stranger to call themselves my student immediately. We should first get to know one another, understand each other's views, and decide whether there is a basis for further discussion.

If This Interests You

Write to me and tell me a little about yourself: which philosophical questions concern you, what ideas of your own you are developing, and why you are interested in helping build a philosophical school. You can begin with one question or one argument. What matters is the substance of your thought, not a grand introduction.

Perhaps our first conversation will mark the beginning of a philosophical school that we will develop together.
